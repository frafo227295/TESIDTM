\section[The Poultry Sector]{Company Overview and the Unique Realities of the Poultry Sector}

Amadori S.p.A. represents a premier model of a fully closed, vertically integrated agri-food enterprise within the European zootechnical sector. Unlike traditional manufacturing paradigms where raw materials can be procured on-demand from global markets to buffer against fluctuating consumer pull, the poultry supply chain is strictly bound by unyielding biological cycles. The corporate integration matrix spans the entire livestock lifecycle under a unified governance framework, encompassing feed mills, parent stock breeding farms, incubation hatcheries, broiler growing farms, primary processing facilities, and downstream logistics networks. While this total vertical control guarantees biosecurity, food safety, and rigorous end-to-end traceability, it introduces structural rigidities and temporal decoupling that heavily complicate demand planning.

The operational dynamics of the poultry sector are governed by an inelastic, upstream biological lead time. Initiating an increase in production volume requires expanding parent stock hatcheries and waiting for biological growth, incubation phases, and broiler fattening periods—a process that unfolds over several months. Consequently, the volume of livestock entering processing facilities on any given day is effectively predetermined by decisions made weeks or months prior. This biological inertia creates acute vulnerabilities when confronted with the fast-moving modern retail sector, which is characterized by volatile promotional campaigns and sudden demand shifts.

Furthermore, while traditional manufacturing follows an additive, assembly-oriented paradigm—where discrete parts are combined into a single product—poultry processing operates as a divergent, subtractive reverse supply chain. The processing plant acts as a disassembly engine, taking a single biological input (the live bird) and breaking it down into a highly differentiated array of co-products, ranging from premium anatomical cuts (breasts, fillets) to secondary industrial inputs. This introduces the complex operational challenge of the carcass mass-balance (\textit{bilancio di massa}). Within the market, consumer demand is rarely uniform across the entire bird; a commercial promotion may cause a sharp spike in demand for chicken breast fillets while demand for wings or drumsticks remains completely flat. Because the facility cannot harvest breast meat without generating proportional volumes of secondary cuts, the disassembly line creates systemic structural surpluses. Managing this balance requires advanced predictive capabilities to align fixed biological inflows with highly asymmetrical commercial demand.

\section[The Complexity of the BOM]{From Neutral to Finished Product: The Complexity of the Bill of Materials}

To manage the structural friction between fixed biological inflows and divergent finished product outputs, the enterprise utilizes a multi-level, hierarchical Bill of Materials (BOM). The structural complexity of this BOM stems from the fact that a single biological asset can be routed through an almost infinite variation of cutting, secondary processing (e.g., breading, seasoning, cooking), marinating, and packaging configurations. To prevent systemic data fragmentation and eliminate predictive noise across tens of thousands of volatile finished stock-keeping units (SKUs), the supply chain architecture introduces a critical abstraction level: the \textit{Neutral Article} (\textit{Articolo Neutro}).

The multi-level BOM is split into two distinct operational phases: the upstream primary disassembly phase and the downstream final differentiation phase. In the primary phase, raw inputs are transformed into stable, generic sub-assemblies—such as bulk deboned breast meat or standardized whole carcasses—completely decoupled from specific customer branding, seasoning variants, or final packaging constraints. These sub-assemblies constitute the neutral product tier. By aggregating demand at this neutral level, statistical forecasting models can isolate stable historical consumption patterns, bypassing the extreme volatility and short lifecycles associated with individual finished customer items, especially for low-rotating products (\textit{Articoli Basso Rotanti}).

The final tier of the BOM governs the final differentiation phase, where the neutral sub-assembly is exploded into consumer-facing finished SKUs through secondary processing and localized packaging operations (such as modified atmosphere trays, private-label branding, or weight-variable configurations). This structural differentiation occurs at the last possible node of the production network, a supply chain strategy known as postponement. When demand baselines are generated at the macro-neutral level, downstream procedural engines explode these volumes through the multi-level BOM to calculate exact requirements for packaging films, ingredients, and processing line capacities. Centralizing this BOM taxonomy within a decoupled semantic architecture is vital: if the underlying relationship between the neutral product and the finished SKU is siloed within fragmented database views, real-time simulation becomes impossible. 

\section[The Planning Cycle]{The Planning Cycle: Demand Forecasting vs. Demand Planning}

Operating successfully within this biological and structural matrix requires a rigorous, multi-stage planning cycle. A critical conceptual distinction must be maintained between the technical execution of the \textit{Demand Forecast} and the strategic orchestration of the \textit{Demand Planning} process. Although frequently conflated in legacy organizational models, these two phases serve entirely different functions, operating at different levels of abstraction and data velocity within the corporate governance loop.

The \textit{Demand Forecast} represents the unconstrained, purely statistical estimation of future market realities. It is an algorithmic exercise that utilizes historical sales records, reconditioned actuals, and deterministic exogenous variables (such as holiday calendars and moving events) to project organic baseline consumption. The current ARIMAX engine discussed in Chapter 3 represents the core of this phase, computing independent forecasts for every unique customer-product combination over a rolling 16-week planning horizon. The objective of the Demand Forecast is mathematical objectivity; it is designed to be free from human bias, commercial optimism, or operational constraints, providing a clean, data-driven baseline of organic market pull.

Conversely, \textit{Demand Planning} is a collaborative, cross-functional business process that ingests the statistical Demand Forecast as its foundational raw input and transforms it into an actionable corporate plan. During this phase, human planners, category managers, and commercial directors layer qualitative business intelligence on top of the statistical baseline. This includes factoring in upcoming commercial promotions, strategic marketing campaigns, new product introductions (NPI), and competitor activities. Furthermore, the Demand Planning process reconciles this commercial demand-push with the operational and biological constraints of the supply chain—verifying whether the projected volumes violate processing plant capacities, distribution limits, or the anatomical carcass mass-balance. 

The output of the Demand Planning cycle is the finalized, constrained \textit{Consensus Plan}, which directly drives the downstream procurement of packaging materials, feed scheduling, hatchery allocations, and daily plant operations. Within a volatile agri-food ecosystem, the separation and fluid connection of these two phases is paramount. By ensuring that the statistical forecast is decoupled from the collaborative planning layer, the enterprise can isolate organic market signals from commercial interventions. This structural clarity allows the Data Office to deliver a trustworthy predictive Data Product to the business, empowering planners to run agile, multi-scenario simulations and quickly recalculate materials requirements via the hierarchical BOM whenever market conditions shift.
